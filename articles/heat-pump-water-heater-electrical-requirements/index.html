Does a Heat Pump Water Heater Need a 240V Circuit

Published 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The unit I installed runs on a 240V circuit and needs an open 30-amp breaker slot near the water heater. Without one, add $300 to $600 for electrical work. The compressor draws little; the 30 amps are sized for the backup resistance elements.

Updated 2026-10-28

This page covers only the electrical side of the swap. The running costs, the noise and the recovery time are in the one year heat pump water heater review.

What my install actually needed

The heat pump water heater replaced a 50-gallon natural gas tank. The gas line came out. A 240V circuit went in. The plumber needed about four hours, and the electrical hookup was straightforward only because there was an open 30-amp breaker slot near the install location. If the panel has no spare slot, or the cable run is long, budget $300 to $600 extra.

That is the number to check before you buy anything: is there a spare breaker position with enough capacity, and is it near enough to the tank.

Why a 30-amp circuit is bigger than the compressor needs

A 30-amp, 240V circuit carries 7,200W of capacity. The National Electrical Code limits a continuous load to 80 percent of the breaker rating, which leaves 5,760W usable.

  • 30A × 240V = 7,200W of circuit capacity
  • 7,200W × 0.8 = 5,760W continuous load

The heat pump compressor does not draw that. The backup resistance elements do. The unit has three modes, and the resistance-only mode runs the tank like a traditional electric water heater. In my first year that mode cost about $35 a month, against $12 a month in heat pump only mode. The circuit is sized for the mode that pulls the most current, not the mode you run every day.

This is also why a heat pump water heater does not let you shrink the breaker. The compressor is efficient. The element that saves you when the compressor is off is not.

Can you run one on a standard 120V outlet

Some models are built for a 120V outlet, and they are worth understanding before you plan the job. A 120V circuit at 15 amps carries 1,800W, which is 1,440W continuous at the same 80 percent rule. That is a quarter of the capacity of the 240V circuit, so a 120V unit recovers much more slowly and relies on a smaller tank. It avoids the electrician if a dedicated outlet already exists, which is the whole point of that design.

Two consequences follow. First, tank size matters more, not less, because recovery is slower. The tank I bought holds 65 gallons, replacing a 50-gallon gas tank, and the extra capacity exists precisely because the unit refills heat slowly. Second, do not assume a 120V model fits your household until you have read its recovery numbers for your hot water peak.

Check the panel, not the brochure

Before you buy, get an electrician to look at the panel and count the large loads. The same check decides an EV charger install, and our EV charging guide walks through the 100-amp versus 200-amp case, including load management devices that cost $200 to $400 and avoid a panel upgrade.

Then read the installation manual for the exact model you are buying. The circuit requirement is stated there, in amps and volts, and it is the only number your electrician needs. A spec sheet that says "standard 240V" without an amperage is not enough.

One more thing the electrician will not tell you

The unit produces condensate the same way a dehumidifier does, so it needs a drain. My basement had a floor drain eight feet away and the line was short. Without a nearby drain, budget $80 to $120 for a condensate pump. That is plumbing, not electrical, but it is part of the same visit and it is easy to forget when you compare quotes.
